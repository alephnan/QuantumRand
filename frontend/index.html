Quantum vs. Classical Random Number Generation

Overview

This application compares random number generation using quantum computing principles (via Qiskit) with classical pseudo-random number generation (via Python's random module).

Quantum Randomness: Fundamentally Non-Deterministic

Quantum random number generation leverages the inherent uncertainty in quantum mechanics formalized in the Born rule. When a quantum system in superposition is measured, the probability of measuring a specific state \(|i\rangle\) is given by:

\[P(|i\rangle) = |\langle i | \psi \rangle|^2\]

In our implementation, we place qubits in a uniform superposition state using Hadamard gates. For a single qubit:

\[H|0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)\]

According to the postulates of quantum mechanics, measurement will yield either 0 or 1 with exactly 50% probability. For \(n\) qubits, we have \(2^n\) possible outcomes, each with equal probability \(2^{-n}\). The measurement process constitutes a non-deterministic collapse, making quantum random numbers theoretically true sources of randomness (subject to hardware limitations).

Classical Randomness: Deterministic Algorithms

In contrast, classical pseudo-random number generators (PRNGs) like the Mersenne Twister algorithm used in Python's random module are deterministic sequences with mathematical properties that simulate randomness. Given a fixed seed value, they will always produce the same sequence of numbers, calculable by the recursion relation:

\[x_{k+n} = f(x_{k+1}, x_{k+2}, ..., x_{k+n-1})\]

While these algorithms are suitable for many applications, they are fundamentally deterministic and therefore not truly random by the definitions of probability theory.

Statistical Comparison

Statistical Testing Framework

We employ distinct statistical tests depending on the data type:

Chi-Square Test for Categorical Data (Bit Patterns)

For bit patterns, we use Pearson's Chi-Square test to evaluate whether the observed frequencies \(O_i\) from the quantum generator differ significantly from the expected frequencies \(E_i\) derived from the classical generator.

The chi-square statistic is calculated as:

\[\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}\]

This statistic approximately follows a chi-square distribution with \(k-1\) degrees of freedom (where \(k\) is the number of categories) under the null hypothesis that the distributions are identical.

The p-value represents the probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one calculated, assuming the null hypothesis is true. Mathematically, it is defined as:

\[p = P(\chi^2_{k-1} \geq \chi^2_{observed})\]

By "convention", a p-value \(< 0.05\) is considered statistically significant evidence to reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the distributions differ.

Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test for Continuous Data (Integers)

For integer distributions, we employ the two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, which compares the empirical cumulative distribution functions \(F_1(x)\) and \(F_2(x)\) of the two samples.

The KS statistic is defined as the maximum absolute difference between these distributions:

\[D_{n,m} = \sup_x |F_{1,n}(x) - F_{2,m}(x)|\]

Where \(n\) and \(m\) are the respective sample sizes. For large samples, the null distribution of this statistic can be approximated and used to calculate a p-value.

This non-parametric test is particularly appropriate for comparing probability distributions without making assumptions about their underlying forms.

Interpretation

A low p-value suggests significant differences between quantum and classical randomness, potentially indicating either:

  1. Quantum generators exhibit true randomness not achievable by classical algorithms
  2. Biases in quantum hardware implementation affecting the output distribution

Conversely, high p-values suggest that for practical purposes, there is no detectable difference between quantum and classical randomness in our implementation, despite their fundamentally different origins.
